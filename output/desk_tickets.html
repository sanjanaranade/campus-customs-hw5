Problem 10 · post-run analysis

Agent-team reflection

Evidence-based review

Overall evaluation

The team completed all three assigned runs and closed tickets 101, 102, and 103 without changing the original database. The strongest evidence is the combination of the three Actual sections, the 27 append-only events in output/audit_trail.json, and the Cash tab’s exact reconciliation from $3,400.00 to $1,000.00. Boss made the expected first routing decision on every ticket; the specialists then handed work to the next appropriate specialist instead of duplicating effort. The main limitation is that “closed” means the operational ticket was resolved with a documented decision: Ticket 101 and Ticket 103 did not execute a restock, purchase order, or external message, so their resolution was an evidence-backed recommendation and draft rather than a completed vendor transaction.

Ticket 101 · performance

Expected versus actual: The plan called for Boss → Inventory → Customer Service. That exact sequence occurred in run f9810392-015f-4865-aabd-e2a5d2a5d2ab. Inventory used get_ticket_restock_and_financial_context for the specific CC-TEE-WHITE, size S shortage and then list_restock_vendor_options to evaluate recorded vendor choices. Customer Service received the findings and left a customer update as a draft, consistent with the no-contact rule.

Evidence and judgment: The Ticket 101 tab records zero stock, Invoice 501 open for $840 to Bulldog Print Co., and a one-unit request from Tauhid Zaman. The Actual section confirms those two context tools, the delegation handoff, and a closed status. This was effective because the team did not invent a supplier or promise availability; it preserved a truthful customer-facing draft while keeping cash unchanged at $3,400 during this run.

Ticket 102 · performance

That restraint was correct under the approval and no-negative-cash rules, although a stronger future version would expose Accounting’s final recommendation and an explicit approval request if a purchase were actually proposed.

What would have been simpler with one tool-using agent?

A single agent with all MCP tools could have completed these three short workflows with fewer handoffs and fewer audit events. For Ticket 101, one agent could read the ticket, call both restock tools, and write the customer draft; for Ticket 102, it could read the lease, check cash/payment status, prepare the $2,400 request, and pause at approval; for Ticket 103, it could inspect the 12-unit shortage, vendors, margin, and cash in one loop. That would reduce routing overhead and make a small queue faster.

However, the multi-agent design created useful separation of duties that the one-agent version would weaken. In Ticket 102, Facilities interpreted lease facts while Accounting handled money, and the human approval gate remained visible. In Tickets 101 and 103, Inventory owned SKU/size and vendor reasoning while Customer Service or Accounting received a focused handoff. The three Actual sections and audit trail make those boundaries inspectable. Therefore one agent is simpler for low-volume, low-risk work, but the current team is safer and more teachable for rent, purchasing, customer communication, and future scale.

Three future problems the team can solve

  1. Another out-of-stock size: Inventory can use the same restock context and vendor-option tools demonstrated by Ticket 101’s exact SKU/size investigation; Customer Service can keep the availability message as a draft.
  2. A later rent or lease discrepancy: Facilities can compare the lease and due-date facts like Ticket 102, while Accounting checks payment history, cash, and prepares an approval-gated payment or escalation.
  3. A larger club order with a discount request: Inventory can quantify shortage and lead time as in Ticket 103; Accounting can evaluate cost, list price, margin, cash, and a purchase proposal before any human-approved commitment.

These are not hypothetical capabilities detached from the app: each directly reuses an agent handoff, an MCP context query, an append-only audit event, and a board tab pattern already exercised in the three runs.

Three problems the current team cannot solve alone

  1. Actual customer or vendor communication: The team can draft Ticket 101’s update, but it cannot safely verify tone, consent, delivery address, or send an email/call. Customer Service plus a human communications workflow would help; the current draft-only guardrail must remain.
  2. Physical inventory truth: The team can reason from recorded counts like Ticket 103’s 8 hoodies and 12-unit shortage, but it cannot count a shelf, inspect defects, or confirm a shipment. Inventory plus a barcode/warehouse tool and a human physical check would be required.
  3. Unusual financial/legal disputes: Accounting can handle the structured $2,400 rent payment in Ticket 102, but it cannot independently negotiate a lease, resolve a disputed invoice, authorize a new bank account, or provide legal advice. Accounting, Facilities, a richer document/approval tool, and human finance/legal review would be needed.

The limitation is deliberate: the current MCP server exposes recorded database facts and controlled payment preparation, not broad external access. Expanding tools without those additional humans, permissions, and evidence checks would undermine the approval, no-negative-balance, no-contact, and audit requirements visible in the Cash and Actual tabs.

Final reflection

The clearest success is controlled coordination: every Boss decision matched the pre-run plan, every specialist contributed a ticket-specific function, drafts stayed on the board, and only the approved rent payment changed cash. The clearest improvement is richer completion semantics for inventory and bulk-order tickets: Ticket 101 and Ticket 103 are closed operationally, but future runs should make the recommendation, approval state, and any blocked vendor action even more explicit before marking them resolved. The dashboard, desk tabs, Cash tab, resolved JSON, screenshots, and append-only audit trail together make that distinction reviewable rather than hiding it behind a generic “done.”
